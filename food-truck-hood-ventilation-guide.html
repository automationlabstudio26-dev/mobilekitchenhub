Food truck hood, ventilation and fire-suppression basics

Hood and suppression requirements depend on the appliances, fuels, cooking processes, installation geometry, listing instructions, and the rules enforced where the unit will operate. Treat airflow figures from generic articles as rough discussion points—not construction specifications.

Start with the menu and equipment schedule. Give the hood designer the manufacturer, model, fuel, heat output, dimensions, and intended cooking process for every appliance beneath or near the hood.

Grease-producing vs. heat or steam loads

Cooking that produces grease-laden vapors typically requires a grease-rated exhaust system and coordinated fire suppression. Equipment producing mainly heat, condensation, or steam may be treated differently. The authority having jurisdiction makes the final determination.

Capture, exhaust, and makeup air

A successful system must capture the cooking plume, exhaust it safely, and replace the removed air without creating uncomfortable drafts or starving combustion appliances. The truck’s small enclosure makes air balance especially important. Hood length alone is not enough to determine airflow.

Fire-suppression coordination

  • Use a qualified fire-suppression contractor for design, installation, testing, and service.
  • Coordinate appliance placement and protected hazard areas before fabrication.
  • Confirm required fuel or electrical shutdown functions.
  • Keep inspection, testing, and maintenance documentation with the vehicle.
  • Provide the extinguisher types and placements required by the local fire authority.

Common planning failures

  • Changing appliance models after the hood and suppression layout is approved.
  • Insufficient clearances around hot equipment or exhaust discharge.
  • Ignoring makeup air and negative pressure.
  • Routing fuel, electrical, or plumbing systems without coordinating the hood package.
  • Assuming approval in one city automatically applies in another.

Before ordering equipment

Ask the health department, fire marshal, building or mechanical authority, and vehicle regulator which drawings, listings, inspections, and certifications they require. Obtain written approval where possible before the builder closes walls or ceilings.
